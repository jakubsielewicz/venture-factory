# Verification — APP 1.7 ADM Transparency "why now" claims

**Status:** decision-support research, NOT legal advice. Every verdict below should still be sign-off-checked by a qualified AU privacy lawyer before any client-facing claim is made. Web access was available for this pass (WebSearch/WebFetch); a small number of pages (AustLII s6D full text, the OAIC ADM Issues Paper PDF itself) returned 403/unreadable-binary and are cited via secondary sources that themselves quote or paraphrase the primary text — flagged per-claim below.

## (a) Commencement date, mechanism, and disclosure content — CONFIRMED

- **Date:** 10 December 2026. Confirmed across the OAIC's own consultation page, multiple law-firm alerts (Allens, MinterEllison, Bird & Bird, HSF Kramer, Norton Rose Fulbright, JWS, Landers, Terralex), and consultancy content (IIS Partners, Helios Salinger, FMA Consulting).
- **Mechanism:** it is a genuinely new set of sub-clauses **APP 1.7, 1.8 and 1.9** (not a differently-numbered APP) inserted into APP 1 ("open and transparent management of personal information") by the **Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 (Cth)**, which received assent Dec 2024 and gave APP entities a ~24-month lead time to the Dec 2026 commencement. This matches the operator's framing exactly.
- **Trigger test (three-part, conjunctive):** (i) the entity has arranged for a computer program to make, or do a thing "substantially and directly related to making," a decision; (ii) that decision could reasonably be expected to **significantly affect** the individual's rights or interests; (iii) personal information is used in the operation of that computer program. All three must be met — this is a narrower gate than "any software touches a decision," and it is exactly the kind of judgment call an inventory exercise is needed to make.
- **What must be disclosed** is NOT a single generic "we use ADM" line — it is per-category detail: (1) the *kinds* of personal information used in such computer programs, (2) the *kinds* of decisions made solely by such programs, and (3) the *kinds* of decisions to which such programs substantially and directly contribute. This is closer to the operator's "per-decision-type" framing than a boilerplate paragraph, which matters for the wedge thesis (a one-line policy add-on plausibly under-discloses).
- **Enforcement:** once in force, the OAIC's infringement-notice and compliance-notice powers (which themselves commenced 10–11 Dec 2024) extend to a privacy policy that fails APP 1.7–1.9, plus mid-tier civil penalty exposure.
- Sources: OAIC consultation page (oaic.gov.au/engage-with-us/consultations/consultation-on-guidance-for-transparency-in-automated-decision-making); Allens "What APP entities need to know about the APP 1 amendments" (Jun 2026); Bird & Bird "Australia's new ADM transparency obligation — OAIC signals a broad reading" (2026); JWS "Practical implications of the new transparency requirements for automated decision making" (2026, quoting the Explanatory Memorandum).

## (b) Health-provider capture regardless of the $3M small-business exemption — CONFIRMED

- The general small-business exemption (turnover ≤ $3M/yr, s6D Privacy Act 1988) already carves out **health service providers** — they are APP entities regardless of turnover. This is a pre-existing feature of the Act (not something new created by the 2024 amendment), confirmed on the OAIC's own "Small business" guidance page (oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/organisations/small-business), which lists "health service providers" first among the categories that must comply regardless of turnover, alongside entities that trade in personal information, Commonwealth contractors, credit reporting bodies, AML/CTF reporting entities, etc. A secondary source (ClinicComply, healthcare-vendor content) cites this specifically as **s6D(4)** — internally consistent with AustLII's listing of s6D as the small-business definition section, but I could not independently re-verify the exact subsection number because AustLII returned a 403 on direct fetch; treat the subsection pinpoint as **PARTIALLY CONFIRMED** (the substantive rule is confirmed via OAIC primary guidance; the exact subsection citation is secondary-sourced).
- **The important nuance the operator's brief glossed over, checked and closed out:** does this old carve-out *automatically* extend to the *new* APP 1.7–1.9 obligation? Yes — because the small-business exemption operates at the level of "is this entity an APP entity at all" (i.e., is it covered by the Privacy Act), not at the level of "which specific APPs apply to it." A health service provider that is already an APP entity (regardless of turnover) is subject to *all* APPs, including the newly inserted APP 1.7–1.9, the same way it is subject to APP 1.1–1.6 today. There is no separate carve-out logic needed for the new sub-clauses — this is standard statutory drafting (new obligations attach to existing "APP entity" status), and no source suggested otherwise. **CONFIRMED**, not merely assumed.
- Aged-care providers are health service providers under this definition (personal/health-care services), so the operator's "every aged-care provider, however small" framing holds.

## (c) OAIC's Jan 2026 ~60-org compliance sweep and general enforcement powers — PARTIALLY CONFIRMED / MATERIAL CORRECTION

- The sweep itself is real, dated, and its scale is correct: **OAIC's first-ever privacy compliance sweep began the first week of January 2026**, reviewing **~60 organisations'** privacy policies. Source: OAIC media release "Privacy compliance sweep to put privacy policies under the spotlight" (oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/privacy-compliance-sweep-to-put-privacy-policies-under-the-spotlight) plus corroborating coverage (MinterEllison, Russell Kennedy, Moores, Mi3, natlawreview.com).
- **Material correction:** this sweep tests **APP 1.4** (general privacy-policy content requirements) — it has nothing to do with the ADM transparency obligation (APP 1.7–1.9), which doesn't even commence until 11 months later. And its six targeted sectors are **rental/property, chemists/pharmacists, licensed venues, car rental, car dealerships, and pawnbrokers/second-hand dealers** — businesses that collect information in person. **Health and aged care are not among the sectors named.** The operator's brief implicitly uses this sweep as evidence OAIC is "coming for" ADM/health-aged-care specifically; that reading is not supported by the primary source. It is fair evidence that OAIC has *shifted its general enforcement posture from guidance to active testing*, which is relevantly true — but it is not sector- or obligation-specific evidence for this venture.
- I separately checked a claim circulating in vendor content (ClinicComply, a healthcare compliance-software blog) that "healthcare is named in the OAIC's 2025-26 regulatory priorities as a high-risk sector" — **UNVERIFIED / likely overstated**, and contradicted by the primary source. The OAIC's actual Corporate Plan 2025-26 regulatory priorities name **rental/property, credit reporting, data brokerage, ad-tech (pixel tracking), AI-driven access/privacy erosion, facial recognition/biometrics, and location-tracking surveillance tech** as focus sectors/technologies — health/aged care is not called out. This is a useful, concrete example of exactly the kind of confidently-plausible-but-fabricated claim the task asked me to guard against, and it is circulating in the wild among vendors targeting this same buyer segment. Flag this for the venture: **do not repeat the "OAIC has named healthcare a priority sector" line** — it is not supported and could itself become a credibility liability if a prospect checks it.
- **Enforcement powers/penalties for a non-compliant privacy policy specifically (as distinct from a data breach), confirmed via OAIC's own regulatory-guide + Clyde & Co / MinterEllison analysis of the three-tier civil penalty regime introduced by the 2024 amendment:** infringement notices of **200 penalty units (~$66,000 for a listed/large body corporate; ~$19,800 for other bodies corporate) per contravention**, escalating to mid-tier civil penalties up to **2,000 penalty units (~$660,000)** for interferences with privacy that don't meet the "serious" threshold, without OAIC needing full litigation. This tier — not the top "serious interference" tier (up to the greater of $50M/30% of turnover/3× benefit) — is the one that attaches to a defective privacy policy, including one that fails APP 1.7–1.9.

## (d) "Not grandfathered" — decisions on/after 10 Dec 2026 regardless of system deployment date — CONFIRMED

- Confirmed via secondary sources paraphrasing the Explanatory Memorandum / OAIC issues paper (MinterEllison "OAIC seeks feedback on automated decision making"; Bird & Bird): the new APP 1 obligations apply **"regardless of whether the arrangement for a computer program to make the decision was made before or after commencement... and regardless of whether the personal information... was acquired before or after commencement."** In plain terms: a legacy system deployed in 2015 is in scope from 10 Dec 2026 onward for any decision it makes from that date, with no carve-out for "we built this years ago." This is the correct, non-grandfathered reading and matches the operator's claim precisely.

## (e) Scope reaches predictive analytics / algorithmic sorting / legacy macros via a "substantially and directly related" test — CONFIRMED (broader than a simple yes/no; the threshold nuance matters commercially)

- **Confirmed as the actual statutory test**, quoting JWS's summary of the Explanatory Memorandum: "substantially" = the computer program's output is "a key factor in facilitating human decision-making"; "directly" = "a direct connection with making the decision." The EM's own worked example (also independently reported via MinterEllison) is a **spreadsheet formula**: converting a date of birth to an age in Excel is "directly related" but not "substantially related" (out of scope); a **pre-programmed Excel formula that scores and triages calls** (e.g., a domestic-violence hotline triage tool) where the score is a key factor in prioritisation **is** "substantially and directly related" (in scope). This is a primary-source-grounded confirmation that the test reaches simple rule-based spreadsheet/macro tooling, not just AI/ML systems — the operator's "even legacy macros" framing is accurate, not hyperbole.
- **IIS Partners' own Jan 2026 client note goes further** and explicitly names "algorithmic sorting or legacy macros" as potentially in scope, matching the operator's language closely (though this is a consultancy's interpretation, not the primary text itself — treat as corroborating, not independently primary).
- **Materially relevant, and worth surfacing because it complicates the sales pitch:** the second limb of the test — "could reasonably be expected to **significantly affect** the individual's rights or interests" — is a real narrowing gate, not a rubber stamp. The OAIC's own Issues Paper (18 May 2026, reported via Pinsent Masons/Out-Law) explicitly names decisions about **government benefits/housing, immigration, financial/insurance products, and healthcare access/treatment prioritisation** as the kind of decisions that clear this bar — algorithmic content ranking or a low-stakes internal scheduling tool plausibly would not. **The OAIC's own Issues Paper uses a fictional aged-care insurer eligibility-decision example** to explore where the line falls (human staff retain final say but rely on AI-generated summaries/recommendations) — a directly on-point, primary-source signal that the regulator itself is thinking about this exact scenario in this exact sector. This is genuinely useful evidence the "inventory needs real judgment, not a checkbox" pitch is sound — but it also means a defensible inventory deliverable requires applying a two-part legal test correctly, which is the crux of the licensed-advice question below.

## Licensed-advice boundary risk

**Verdict: manageable-with-framing, not clear.**

Existing non-law-firm AU privacy consultancies already operate in this exact space commercially: IIS Partners (automation mapping/inventory, materiality-threshold assessment, "privacy policy updates for ADM transparency"), Helios Salinger (formerly/related to Salinger Privacy — Privacy Impact Assessments, Algorithmic Impact Assessments, gap analysis, and a "template Privacy Policy" + "template Privacy Risk Assessment Questionnaire" sold inside "Compliance Kits"), and FMA Consulting (a four-phase Audit/Classify/Explain/Remediate ADM roadmap) all draft or template privacy-policy-adjacent text as a commercial, non-law-firm service today. That is real market precedent that this category of work is broadly accepted as consulting, not reserved legal practice, provided it isn't held out as legal advice.

The risk is more specific than "drafting text." Per how Australian courts and law societies describe unqualified legal practice (Law Society Journal: "the essence of legal practice is the advising of a particular person in a particular situation and the production of a document which affects legal rights, and which is tailored to the particular needs of that person"; Victorian Legal Services Board on "ghost drafting"), the risk concentrates specifically in the **judgment call**, not the drafting mechanics: deciding whether a *specific* client system clears the "substantially and directly related" + "significantly affects rights or interests" two-part test is itself an interpretation of a civil-penalty-backed statutory provision, applied to a specific client's specific facts — closer to legal advice than the drafting of the resulting policy paragraph is. A generic template ("we may use ADM for X") is low risk; a tailored register that says "System Y is/isn't in scope, therefore the policy must/must not disclose it" is the higher-risk artefact, especially at $4,500 fixed-price with no named lawyer reviewing each determination.

**Mitigations that make this manageable, not disqualifying:**
1. Structure the deliverable so the **scope determination (in/out) is presented as a recommendation for the client's own counsel to confirm**, not a final answer the client relies on directly — mirrors how IIS/Helios Salinger position their templates.
2. Route final sign-off through a **named partner law firm** before any drafted text goes live in a client's published privacy policy — this is also the operator's own stated channel idea (boutique law firms) and should be treated as a structural control, not just a nice-to-have GTM channel.
3. Carry **professional indemnity insurance** (not itemised in the operator's $5K capital ceiling — flagged as a gap below).
4. Engagement letter/ToS explicit that the register and drafted sections are "documentation to support the client's own compliance decision and legal review," consistent with existing market framing.

None of this makes the risk zero — the fixed-price, high-volume model (many clients, same $4,500 deliverable, fast turnaround) creates pressure to cut the "route to a lawyer" step that keeps this on the right side of the line. That tension is a genuine G1 unknown, not resolved here.
